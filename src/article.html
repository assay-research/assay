What the leaderboards hide

We screened all 47,190 accounts on the public Hyperliquid leaderboard, measured 317 of them trade by trade, and found that 143 survive a test the leaderboard itself does not apply. Then we asked the question none of the copy-trading products answer: with a $200 account, how many of those 143 could you actually follow? Thirty-two. Seven at $99.

Method and raw counts below No affiliation with Hyperliquid or any copy-trading product Not investment advice

Every copy-trading product built on a public order book starts from the same place: rank the wallets, show the curve, offer a button. The ranking is the product. So the ranking is where to look, and the ranking is built on data with six specific, checkable defects.

None of what follows is an opinion about anyone's trading. It is arithmetic on the exchange's own API, and each finding below names the number you can reproduce it from.

Finding 1

Two in five of the accounts we had to throw out were dead ones still being ranked

274 of 656 Exclusions that were accounts with no BTC trade in over 45 days — 41.8%, the single largest data-quality problem in the exercise.

They are not delisted. They sit in the table with a month-return figure attached, and some of those figures are large: four of them displayed 112.9%, 207.6%, 122.7% and 173.0% month returns after more than a hundred days without placing a trade. The number is real. It is just not about this month, and nothing on the page says so.

Staleness is about 5% across every account we screened, but roughly 10% in the top 40 by return. It concentrates exactly where the ranking is pointing, because an account that stopped trading stopped losing.

Finding 2

The best-looking record on the chain is half a position

606 closed trades, 0 losses $3.43m realised, 100% taker across all 1,318 fills, BTC and ETH only.

This is the account anyone ranking by return or by win rate finds first. We checked whether it was hiding a loss somewhere: it is not. $3.51m of perpetual equity, $29.85m of notional, one open 12,000 ETH position at 20× showing +$597,506 unrealised.

A discretionary trader does not go 606 for 606. The readings that fit are an arbitrage or basis book whose other leg is not on this exchange, or a book that only ever realises the profitable tranche. Either way the on-chain record is one side of a position, and copying one leg of a hedge takes on precisely the directional risk the hedge exists to cancel.

It is not a lone case. 125 of the accounts we measured had no losing trade at all. And the exchange reports closedPnl, which is realised only, so an account can carry an underwater position indefinitely and show a clean record the whole time.

Finding 3

Ranking by win rate is a known mistake. Ranking by edge fails the same way.

Win rate alone says nothing, which everyone knows, so the sophisticated version is to compare it against the win rate a trader needs to break even:

break-even % = avg_loss / (avg_win + avg_loss), and edge is win % − break-even %.

That is the right formula and it still produced nonsense, because of what it is estimated from. The top twelve accounts by edge scored +86 to +98 points. Their loss counts:

1  1  1  1  5  2  1  2  2  7  3  1 Losing trades behind each of the twelve largest measured edges. Median: two. Eight of the twelve have exactly one.

A break-even rate of 0.5% means the average loss is a two-hundredth of the average win, which happens when one small loss is the entire loss sample. An edge estimated from a single loss is noise with a formula on it.

Requiring at least ten losing trades before the number is allowed to count takes the "profitable" population from 205 down to 143. Their median edge is +27.1 points. That is the honest version of the same list.

Finding 4

Every one of these products shows you a window and calls it a career

2,000 fills The maximum userFills returns. Median span of the history we could see: 124 days.

The API hands back the most recent 2,000 fills and no more. For a quiet account that is a long history; for an active one it is a few weeks, and the part you cannot see is the earlier part, which is where a trader was learning. Two accounts in our measurable set were sitting exactly on the cap. Nothing on a leaderboard distinguishes a 90-day record from a three-year one.

Finding 5

The accounts that blew up are not in the pool at all

52.8% Share of the 43,356 accounts with a usable window that are profitable all-time — measured after the liquidated ones have already gone.

Liquidated accounts do not appear on the leaderboard. Whatever you compute from it is computed on survivors, so every win rate, every return and every edge in this article is an overestimate, including ours. A barely-positive coin flip is the honest reading of that 52.8%, and it is the optimistic reading.

Finding 6

Your account size decides this, not the trader's skill — and nobody tells you

This is the finding with money attached, and it is the one we have not seen anywhere else.

When you copy someone, the order placed for you is their_order × (your_equity / their_equity). The traders on these leaderboards hold six figures; the median equity among our 143 is $166,930. Scale their position down to a $200 account and a large share of the orders land under the exchange minimum and never go out at all.

So we counted it. Of the 143 that survived every test above, how many could a given account actually follow — defined as at least 80% of their orders placeable at your size, and not one blocked exit:

And the smaller your account, the worse the traders you can reach: the best edge available at $99 is +25.4 against +70.1 at the top of the list, and the seven include win rates of 16.1% and 23.7%. The followable ones are not the good ones.

The part that should be on every product page

A blocked order is survivable on the way in. You simply do not get the trade. A blocked order on the way out leaves you holding leveraged exposure that nobody is managing — the trader has closed, your copy has not, and no further signal is coming.

Of the 143, 125 have at least one order a $99 account could not place to get out. At $400 it is still 85. The median $99 account could place about 59.7% of a survivor's orders. That is the number that decides whether copying works, and it is a property of your balance, not of their skill.

What we do with this, and what we will not do

We publish the data on one address and let you read it. We do not rank traders, we do not tell you who to copy or at what size, and we never place an order. The moment a service says "copy this one, at this size" it is giving a personal recommendation on a financial instrument, and if it executes for you it is managing your money. Both are regulated activities in the EU and both are somebody else's business.

So the tool is deliberately narrower than the products it is a check on. One address in, one page out: is it stale, is its equity representative, how many losses is its edge estimated from, what share of its orders could you place at your size, and are any of its exits blocked.

Method

Everything above comes from two public Hyperliquid endpoints and no paid data. The leaderboard is a plain GET from stats-data.hyperliquid.xyz; per-account state and fills come from POST api.hyperliquid.xyz/info. Fills were pulled once for roughly a thousand addresses and cached, so the whole measurement re-runs without touching the API again.

A trade is a closed BTC perpetual position as the exchange reports it, net of the fees in the same records. Equity is the account's current perpetual and spot value. Order placeability is computed per order, scaling the trader's size by the equity ratio and testing the result against the exchange minimum; a blocked exit is a closing order that fails that test.

Limits of this work, stated rather than buried

  • It is a window, not a career. 2,000 fills maximum, as in finding 4.
  • The pool is survivorship-biased before we start, as in finding 5. Our 143 is an upper bound.
  • Equity is today's number applied across the whole history. An account that grew tenfold over the window would have been less followable earlier than we show.
  • The exchange minimum we test against is taken from the published rules and has not been verified by placing live orders. If it is wrong, the counts in finding 6 move.
  • Not one of the 143 has been examined individually. We attacked exactly one of them, the best-looking, and it failed on three further grounds: three losses in 126 trades, BTC only 13% of its activity, and 82% of its orders resting limits a copier would not get filled on. A shortlist is not a recommendation. It is a list of things to attack.
