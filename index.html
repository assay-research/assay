What the leaderboards hide

We screened all 47,190 accounts on the public Hyperliquid leaderboard, then measured them trade by trade across four markets — BTC, ETH, SOL and HYPE. 1,010 records survived long enough to be measurable. 482 pass a test the leaderboard itself does not apply. Then we asked the question none of the copy-trading products answer: with a $200 account, how many of those 482 could you actually follow? Eighty-four. Twenty-eight at $99.

By Assay No affiliation with Hyperliquid or any copy-trading product Not investment advice

Every copy-trading product built on a public order book starts from the same place: rank the wallets, show the curve, offer a button. The ranking is the product. So the ranking is where to look, and the ranking is built on data with six specific, checkable defects.

We measured four markets rather than one so that each finding can be tested against itself. Three of the six turn out to be worse away from BTC. One of them largely disappears on HYPE, and we say so, because a page that asks you to check its numbers does not get to quote only the convenient ones.

None of this is an opinion about anyone's trading. It is arithmetic on the exchange's own API, and each finding names the number you can reproduce it from.

Finding 1

Between two and three in five excluded accounts were dead ones still being ranked

41.8% → 60.2% Share of each market's exclusions that were accounts with no trade in over 45 days. BTC is the mildest case of the four; on SOL it is three in five.

They are not delisted. They sit in the table with a month-return figure attached, and some of those figures are large: four of them displayed 112.9%, 207.6%, 122.7% and 173.0% month returns after more than a hundred days without placing a trade. The number is real. It is just not about this month, and nothing on the page says so.

Staleness is about 5% across every account we screened, but roughly 10% in the top 40 by return. It concentrates exactly where the ranking is pointing, because an account that stopped trading stopped losing.

It is the largest single exclusion category in all four markets, which is what makes it a property of the leaderboard rather than of any one coin.

Finding 2

About one in five excluded accounts has never had a losing trade

19.1%   18.7%   17.1%   19.5% Share of exclusions that were accounts with no loss at all, in BTC, HYPE, ETH and SOL. The most consistent number we measured.

A record with no losses cannot be assessed, because the break-even calculation in Finding 3 needs an average loss to divide by. More importantly, a flawless record is usually a sign that the record is incomplete rather than that the trader is flawless.

The clearest case: one account shows 606 closed trades, not one of them a loss, $3.43m realised, 100% taker across all 1,318 fills. We checked whether it was hiding a loss. It is not — $3.51m of perpetual equity, $29.85m of notional, one open 12,000 ETH position at 20× showing +$597,506 unrealised.

A discretionary trader does not go 606 for 606. The readings that fit are an arbitrage or basis book whose other leg is not on this exchange, or a book that only ever realises the profitable tranche. Either way the on-chain record is one side of a position, and copying one leg of a hedge takes on precisely the directional risk the hedge exists to cancel.

The exchange reports closedPnl, which is realised only, so an account can carry an underwater position indefinitely and show a clean record the whole time.

Finding 3

Ranking by edge fixes the win-rate problem and introduces a new one — in three markets out of four

Win rate alone says nothing, which everyone knows, so the sophisticated version is to compare it against the win rate a trader needs just to break even:

break-even % = avg_loss / (avg_win + avg_loss), and edge is win % − break-even %.

That is the right formula, and on BTC it still produced nonsense — because of what it was estimated from. These are the loss counts behind the twelve largest edges in each market:

On BTC and ETH, eight of the top twelve rest on two losses or fewer. A break-even rate of 0.5% means the average loss is a two-hundredth of the average win, which happens when one small loss is the entire loss sample. An edge estimated from a single loss is noise with a formula on it.

On HYPE the defect mostly is not there, and the reason is visible in the data: HYPE traders close a median of 166 trades against BTC's 76. It is the exchange's own token, traded hardest by its most active accounts, so there is enough history to estimate from. The defect is real, and it is a property of thin records rather than of the formula. Check the loss count, not the coin.

Requiring at least ten losing trades before an edge is allowed to count removes 30% of BTC's "profitable" accounts, 29% of ETH's, 27% of SOL's and 21% of HYPE's — in exactly the order the table above predicts.

Finding 4

Every one of these products shows you a window and calls it a career

2,000 fills The maximum userFills returns, in any market. Median span of the history we could see: 106 days — 124 on BTC, 110 on HYPE, 92 on ETH, 62 on SOL.

The API hands back the most recent 2,000 fills and no more. For a quiet account that is a long history; for an active one it is a few weeks, and the part you cannot see is the earlier part, which is where a trader was learning. Nothing on a leaderboard distinguishes a 90-day record from a three-year one.

This bites hardest exactly where Finding 3 bites least. A HYPE trader closing 166 trades fills the 2,000-fill window fastest, so the deep loss counts that make their edge trustworthy are also the ones most likely to be a recent slice of a longer, different-looking career.

Finding 5

The accounts that blew up are not in the pool at all

52.8% Share of the 43,356 accounts with a usable window that are profitable all-time — measured after the liquidated ones have already gone.

Liquidated accounts do not appear on the leaderboard. Whatever you compute from it is computed on survivors, so every win rate, every return and every edge in this article is an overestimate, including ours. A barely-positive coin flip is the honest reading of that 52.8%, and it is the optimistic reading.

Finding 6

Your account size decides this, not the trader's skill — and nobody tells you

This is the finding with money attached, it is the one we have not seen made anywhere else, and it is the one that got worse the further we looked from BTC.

When you copy someone, the order placed for you is their_order × (your_equity / their_equity). The traders on these leaderboards hold six figures. Scale their position down to a $200 account and a large share of the orders land under the exchange minimum and never go out at all.

So we counted it. Of the 482 records that survived every test above, how many could a given account actually follow — defined as at least 80% of their orders placeable at your size, with no blocked exits:

At $99 you can follow 28 traders out of 482. Six percent. Going from $99 to $400 multiplies the pool more than fivefold, and nothing about the traders changed.

And the smaller your account, the worse the traders you can reach. On BTC the best edge available at $99 is +25.4 against +70.1 at the top of the list. The followable ones are not the good ones.

The part that should be on every product page

A blocked order is survivable on the way in. You simply do not get the trade. A blocked order on the way out leaves you holding leveraged exposure that nobody is managing — the trader has closed, your copy has not, and no further signal is coming.

426 of the 482 — 88% — have at least one order a $99 account could not place to get out. At $400 it is still 316, or 66%. The median survivor's orders are only 45.3% placeable at $99, and on SOL that falls to 36.4%. That is the number that decides whether copying works, and it is a property of your balance, not of their skill.

What we do with this, and what we will not do

We publish the data on one address and let you read it. We do not rank traders, we do not tell you who to copy or at what size, and we never place an order. The moment a service says "copy this one, at this size" it is giving a personal recommendation on a financial instrument, and if it executes for you it is managing your money. Both are regulated activities in the EU and both are somebody else's business.

So the tool is deliberately narrower than the products it is a check on. One address in, one page out: is it stale, is its equity representative, how many losses is its edge estimated from, what share of its orders could you place at your size, and are any of its exits blocked.

Method

Everything above comes from two public Hyperliquid endpoints and no paid data. The leaderboard is a plain GET from stats-data.hyperliquid.xyz; per-account state and fills come from POST api.hyperliquid.xyz/info. Fills were pulled once for roughly three thousand addresses and cached, which is why measuring four markets instead of one cost no further API calls.

A trade is a closed perpetual position in that market as the exchange reports it, net of the fees in the same records. Equity is the account's current perpetual and spot value read together, because perp alone reports zero for anyone parking funds in spot. Order placeability is computed per order, scaling the trader's size by the equity ratio and testing the result against the exchange minimum; a blocked exit is a closing order that fails that test.

Limits of this work, stated rather than buried

  • It is a window, not a career. 2,000 fills maximum, as in Finding 4.
  • The pool is survivorship-biased before we start, as in Finding 5. Our 482 is an upper bound.
  • Equity is today's number applied across the whole history. An account that grew tenfold over the window would have been less followable earlier than we show.
  • The exchange minimum we test against is taken from the published rules and has not been verified by placing live orders. If it is wrong, the counts in Finding 6 move.
  • Not one of the 482 has been examined individually. We attacked exactly one, the best-looking, and it failed on three further grounds: three losses in 126 trades, BTC only 13% of its activity, and 82% of its orders resting limits a copier would not get filled on. A shortlist is not a recommendation. It is a list of things to attack.
  • Four markets on one exchange. BTC, ETH, SOL and HYPE on Hyperliquid, screened 9–13 October 2026. We have not measured any other venue, and nothing here should be assumed to carry across to one.
  • The same address can appear in several markets. 1,010 measurable records come from 682 distinct addresses; the 482 survivors from 388.
